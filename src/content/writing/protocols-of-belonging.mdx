## Our data deserves a home

Most of our data is held captive. Not roughly. Comfortably. It has a residence where it is stored, backed up, and put to use. It is even reasonably accessible, as long as the owner of the residence permits. If we want it out, we can usually get it out. But then it stays out. It is not free to come and go.

We are not sure a home is a home if it asks that of you.

A home is somewhere we belong. Somewhere we are safe. Somewhere we can rest. It is not somewhere we have to stay. We should feel able to leave a home at any time. If we cannot leave it, it is a prison.

That is the test this essay applies, and it applies it to everything, including the things we are building ourselves.

## Three homes

Fritjof Capra and David Steindl-Rast called one of their books *Belonging to the Universe*. The most primordial home is the one we all inhabit. One level down, everyone on Earth belongs to the atmosphere. We are all held in the same blend of nitrogen and oxygen, and nobody owns it. Pope Francis wrote *Laudato Si'* under the subtitle "On Care for Our Common Home." At a time when so much divides us, it is worth saying plainly: we share a home already. Belonging is not something we have to manufacture. At the most basic level, we are in.

Our digital lives should reflect that. Instead they reflect the opposite. Facebook, Instagram, X, TikTok: each is a comfortable residence with billions of people inside, and each is hard to leave for the same reason. The people you love are in there. Your relationships are the moat. If you go somewhere else, you are cut off from the people who made the place worth being in.

Each of these platforms can feel like a home. Each becomes a prison the moment you try the door.

## How the prison got built

It is tempting to tell this as a story about villains. It is more useful to tell it as a story about shape.

A platform bundles four jobs into one object. It gives you an identity. It stores what you make. It keeps the index that decides what can be found. And it owns the only window onto that index, the app. From the outside you see one thing. From the inside, the bundle gives the owner four separate ways to make leaving expensive.

You can download your posts, but the download does not bring your friends. Another app has a better feed, but none of your groups are there. The people remain, but reaching them now runs through a company that charges for the route.

Enclosure only pays when exit is expensive. Exit is expensive when identity, storage, index, and interface are one object. Once a company's business depends on that, every competitor faces the same math, and all of them become more extractive together. That is why good intentions have never been enough. The attention economy was not a moral failure. It was a topology. And a topology is the kind of thing you can change.

Here is the question that changes it: what happens when the four jobs come apart?

Pulling them apart does not settle every question about power. It moves the places where choices get made. We can ask for a different window without asking everyone we know to move into a different world.

## A common language, and who owns it

To belong somewhere, we have to be free to move. And we can only move freely in a place whose language we speak. Most of us would not last long in the Amazon rainforest. People born there speak the forest's language and move through it as home. A hundred years ago, most of us would have struggled on the far side of the globe. Today we find a country that speaks English, or we open a translator, and we manage.

These are protocols. So is the dollar. So is the gong at 4:20 on a Friday at the Hub in Boulder, which turns a working day into a social one. A protocol is a shared set of expectations that lets people act together without a boss in the room. Some are open and let strangers coordinate at scale. Some are closed and hold intimacy, like a handshake between two friends. Neither kind is wrong.

The trouble starts with ownership. English and the dollar became global languages, and they did it by belonging to the dominant power rather than to the people who speak them. Whoever owns the common language keeps setting its terms, and everyone else keeps adapting. Global coordination through a dominator's monoculture is the trap we fell into, and it looks a lot like Facebook.

So here is the claim, in one sentence. A network monopoly is a common language owned by someone other than its speakers. What we want is a common language owned by the people who speak it.

## The atmosphere

Atproto is the most serious attempt we know of to build that for social life.

It is not a home. It is a language. A person's identity and records live with a host they chose, a personal data server, or PDS. Relays pass along what changes. AppViews build the feeds, the search, the conversations, the windows. Any of those windows can be replaced. The part that is hard to recreate, your identity and your history and your people, lives in the layer you hold. The part that is easy to recreate, the index, lives in the layer a company holds.

Changing hosts should be like moving house. You do not become a different person. You do not lose your friends.

<PortabilityLab />

A host that cannot turn its users into hostages has exactly one way to keep them. Be worth staying for.

<details>
<summary>What "moving without permission" actually requires</summary>

The [atproto overview](https://atproto.com/guides/overview) describes identities that point to a host, and signed repositories of records that any host can carry. Migration without the old host's cooperation depends on two things you should not assume just because an account exists: that you control your own rotation key, and that you hold a copy of your data. Both are possible. Neither is automatic. Apps may also depend on specific indexing or moderation services. This is an architectural possibility with operating conditions, not a promise that every move is effortless today.

</details>

The people building on this protocol call the whole ecosystem the ATmosphere. We think the pun is exactly right, and not only because it is charming. An atmosphere is a medium nobody owns and everyone is already inside. You do not join it. You are in it by being alive. And an atmosphere is made by its inhabitants. The oxygen in ours was put there by living things, breath by breath, over two billion years. Every small project that builds on atproto adds to the common air. If the project dies, the records stay where they always were, with the people who made them.

That is the difference between a network you are locked into and one you are held by.

## Techne and oikos: many small centers

An atmosphere is not a house. We still need doors, kitchens, quiet rooms, and someone who notices when we are missing.

The missing tier in most arguments about technology is the household. On one side is the sovereign individual, told to run their own server, which almost nobody wants to do. On the other is the platform, where only capital at civilizational scale can run anything. Between them is where life actually happens: the household, the co-op, the hub, the congregation, the neighborhood.

*Economy* comes from the Greek *oikos*, household, and *nomos*, management. *Ecology* comes from the same root. The attention economy is management with no household in it, a hundred billion dollars of infrastructure built for the home of no one. *Techne* is the Greek word for craft. Put the two together and you have what we are trying to do: the craft of making, in service of a household.

The cost of recreating Facebook is out of reach for any person and probably for any community. But a small group of people who coordinate can own their own infrastructure and plug it into the larger network. A co-op can host its members. My PDS can be my digital home because it is my people hosting it, and I trust them, and I can still leave. Subsidiarity is the PDS. Solidarity is the federation.

<details>
<summary>A boundary is not a wall</summary>

Most of what makes a community a community is not public: membership, money, care, conflict. Until recently atproto could only broadcast, which would have pushed every real community back inside a walled garden for exactly the data that constitutes it. [Atproto Spaces](https://atproto.com/blog/atproto-spaces-alpha), in alpha since August 2026, adds permissioned records that still live on each member's own host. Leave the group and you keep your records. Two honest limits: it is an alpha, and it gives access control, not confidentiality. Anyone the space admits can read what is in it. A membrane, not a wall, and a cell dies as surely from a sealed membrane as from a dissolved one.

</details>

## The test applies here too

It would be easy to apply the prison test to the platforms we dislike and suspend it for the protocol we hope will replace them. So: does atproto belong to the people who speak it?

Not yet. Three places where it does not, and what each one asks of us.

<details>
<summary>Identity. Who says you are you?</summary>

Most atproto identities resolve through a directory that Bluesky, the company, operates. That is the single most centralized point in the stack. In September 2025 Bluesky announced an independent Swiss association to run the directory, set its policies, and hold the intellectual property. As of this writing that is planned, not seated. "Being resolved" is accurate. "Resolved" is not. We intend to be among the communities that help govern it.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Vocabulary. Who decides what a record means?</summary>

Shared schemas let apps read each other's records, and every schema is a decision. What is a group? Is a member a kind of group, or a tag on one? Two of us who agree about nearly everything could not settle that in one conversation. That is the evidence, not the obstacle. A shared minimum needs governing, in the open, the way the internet's own standards are governed. Forking a vocabulary is not governance failing. It is the mechanism working.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Discovery. Who can afford to help us find each other?</summary>

Permission to build an index is not the money to run one. If exactly one index is economically viable, the chokepoint has been rebuilt one layer up, with better exit rights but still a chokepoint. Plurality of indexes is an economic question, not a protocol one, and it is not solved.

</details>

None of this undercuts the argument. It changes what the argument is. Atproto does not pass its own test on its own. It passes if communities like ours show up and help govern the shared minimum. This is a call, not an endorsement.

## What we are building

We are pouring real effort into atproto because we think it is the best available bridge between the network monopoly and the kind of belonging we mean. Not in the abstract. In things that run.

The calendar at cohereboulder.org stores its events as atproto records on a personal data server, through the same system, regenOS, that the Techne Foundation is now releasing as the Techne SDK. regenhub.xyz runs on it too. At the Techne Cooperative we are moving the Hub's own events and member profiles onto the same records. And this essay is a markdown file in a public repository that anyone can download, copy, or carry elsewhere. Publishing it as an atproto record is the next step, and the choice of which lexicon to use is still open.

Small things. They are the test applied to ourselves.

## Something worth staying for

We do not fully agree with each other, and this draft keeps the disagreement visible.

One of us thinks a protocol cannot create belonging. A walled garden does not merely fail to produce belonging; it metabolizes it, turning relationship into engagement. So the protocol's job is to stop eating it, and the rest is practice: meeting, listening, staying through difficulty.

The other thinks technology can reflect and extend life directly. Life is inclusive. At the most fundamental level we already belong, and a system that lets people host one another, carry their history, and shape its rules is not an empty permission. It expresses a relationship.

We have found a way to test which of us is right. Every claim like that has to cash out in a design decision. When someone leaves a cooperative, what happens to their records? When a community changes hosts, who has to consent? Does the system have users, or members? Living systems do not have users. Most software does. That difference is checkable in a schema.

We are not going to out-fund the monopolies. We do not have to. The thing they sell is a home you cannot leave. We have to make it obsolete by making leaving free. Then comes the older and harder work, the work no architecture does for us: building something worth staying for.

Start with a question that fits in ordinary conversation. If we chose to leave, what could we carry with us?

Then the better one. Once staying is a choice, what will we make together that makes us want to?
